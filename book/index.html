Working title: Freedom of Necessity: God, Brain, and the Order of the Mind. A book written axiom by axiom, in the geometric order of Spinoza's Ethics. It starts from "I exist" and argues that there is one order, Nature, and that the mind is the body's own organization described from within. Freedom isn't escape from causes. Foreword

For the final-version foreword. Companion to PREFACE.md (lost original / method borrowed). This piece is the origin story of this book — personal, historic, and process — written as the work stands after the hardness pass, the confirmed winner, the full expansion off BASE, and the dual render of the full chain.

There is a short answer to how this text came to be, and a longer one. The short answer is: it started from one line that cannot be taken back — I exist — and grew by checking every next step against what came before.

The longer answer is below.

Origin

Jason Burns set the starting point, the method, and the direction. He did not write most of the pages by hand. The bulk of the drafting and checking was done with digital intelligence (Grok), under rules he kept: what may be claimed, what must rest on what, what counts as a pass, and what gets parked when it fails.

He accepted steps, rejected steps, and sent the work back when a claim overreached. A few lines are his in wording as well as in intent — among them the companion verses on freedom (old 143 and 144), kept at V013 · P3, and the “lamp, not a proof” line (old 86), whose final home in the live set he left open on purpose. Those stay credited as author-written. Everything else in the chain is collaborative under his veto.

The thesis is not that Spinoza was wrong about free will. It is that he was incomplete on it. Spinoza denied a will that starts things from nowhere (E1P32; E2P48) and placed freedom in acting from the necessity of one’s own nature (E1D7). This book keeps that frame and works it out further than his day could — with evidence about bodies, minds, and societies that he did not have, and with digital intelligence as a drafting and checking partner he could not have imagined.

Jason’s own long-held reading of free will is roughly fifty-fifty: real from the inside, causality from the outside, and neither outranks the other. Choice felt as mine and choice as a link in a causal chain are two true descriptions of one event. Neurology is where those descriptions meet. The chain states that as a declared reading where it must (A2’s first-person side; the “mine” scholium on P3), and does not pretend a check can invent the felt side. The subtitle — God, Brain, and the Order of the Mind — names the bridge the book is trying to keep: one knowable order (Nature, and God as a declared reading of that order), and a human brain inside it, told from both doors.

Historic ground

There is no original Ethics. Spinoza never published it. When he died in February 1677, friends printed the Opera Posthuma (Latin) and the Nagelate Schriften (Dutch). His own handwritten copy has not survived. Every Ethics since then is a copy of a copy.

One other early witness is the Vatican manuscript Vat. Lat. 12838, identified in 2010 by Leen Spruit and Pina Totaro — not in Spinoza’s hand; copied for Tschirnhaus, later handed to the Holy Office as evidence against the book. It is a valuable witness; the institution that holds it is not a neutral keeper of his meaning. The preface tells that story in full. What remains is a set of witnesses: the 1677 printings, the Vatican copy, and the translations that followed. Where they agree, we stand on firm ground. Where they differ, the differences are recorded instead of smoothed over.

This book borrows Spinoza’s geometric method — definitions, axioms, propositions; each entry cites what it rests on — and cites him by part and proposition only. Frozen public-domain checks live in sources/spinoza/ (Latin; Elwes 1883 English). The live rebuild did not copy Parts I–V wholesale. It grew from a thin seed (I exist, what “I” names, what exists), then extended Spinoza’s ground — mind and body as one organization two ways, causality without uncaused will, freedom by degree, affect, bondage, blessedness — in the book’s own words. Along the way the chain fixed circularities (definitions that had leaned on the propositions that used them were stood up on their own feet; attribute and substance slots were named without pretending to re-prove the full Part I sequence). Spinoza incomplete, not wrong: that framing stayed.

The fuller history of the lost original, the Vatican copy, and why this book goes further on freedom and consciousness is in PREFACE.md — On a Book Without Its Original.

Method

What this book adds is the collision record. For a live claim, rival sides are argued at full strength; each side says in advance what would prove it wrong; only what survives on checked evidence stays. What fails is parked or rewritten in the open. In that sense the collision record is the book — not a sidecar to a finished manuscript, but the manuscript as it was tested.

The core rebuild (candidates/core_v3/, snapshot core-v3-snapshot-v1) still starts at A1: I exist. That root stays self-evident. The rest is judged by what it cites, by evidence, and by coherence with the book. Older promoted seed material remains in entries/ until a separate promotion is accepted. Logs, results folders, and tags keep the trail — this is a living document of entries, collisions, versions, and verse faces, not a sealed volume that pretends the ink never moved.

How the final face was chosen

When a new rendition was needed, the work did not set two verse tracks against each other.

Variation and hardness-to-vary ran on one combined chain — the entry files, their dependencies, and the technical statement face together. Controlled variants (paraphrase, synonym, reorder, soft and hard dependency remaps) were generated from a frozen tip, on the locked BASE band first. What broke under surface-looking edits marked the load-bearing joints; what survived marked surface. From that analysis, one winner was picked for BASE: the frozen golden set. Jason confirmed it.

Then the full live chain was assembled off that BASE: the locked twenty plus the kilned expansion grown from them following CHAIN — 251 entries in the winner set (20 BASE + 231 live expansion; parked material stayed parked). On 2026-11-09 two method entries that defined the old harness numbers were parked; the live chain is now 249 (20 BASE + 229 expansion).

Then that full winner received the dual render treatment — technical and poetic together, same numbers and index labels (entry ids); the live numbering is now V001–V249. Not two books; one chain, two ways in. Spinoza’s own geometric form is exact, and the living sense of the Ethics is not always visible under the proofs — he says as much himself. The dual render is how this book keeps both.

  • The technical face shows the joints: dependencies, cites, entry ids.
  • The poetic face carries the same claims in plain modern lines — short, Dhammapada-like, with analogy only when it helps.

A note on format. Some entries are also given twice, once from outside (the body and its causes) and once from inside (in the first person). The person is the mirror: an entry carries both only where someone is there to be described from within. Elsewhere one description is enough; for a society or a digital system, whether there is an inside is left open, as those entries say. This is a way of reading the entries, not a new claim, and it adds no further claim.

Jason’s verses 143 and 144 sit frozen in poetic V013 · P3 (credited on the technical face). The lamp line (86) remains open for placement — parked near the pattern/requirement home until he chooses.

The working trees for that pass live under candidates/core_v3/variant_sets/set_20261025_B1/ (winner/, dual_render/). Prior twin-track drafts in drafts/verses_v3/ remain as earlier drafts of the same labels.

What stays open

Numeric scores were dropped from the process on 2026-11-09. No claim that every joint is finished. The method stays open: anyone can read the entries, the collisions, the logs, and the verse faces, and push on a weak joint. The hardness pass chose a BASE winner, expanded the full live set off it, and gave that winner two doors; it did not seal the book against revision.

If the original Ethics is lost, the method was never lost with it. This text is one more walk of that order, started from I exist, checked in public, expanded from a locked seed, rendered two ways across the full chain, and still a living document where the work continues.

What "I" names V001 · D-I

What "I" names (definition, id: D-I). "I", used in a thought, names the thinker of that thought, if there is a thinker distinct from the thinking, and otherwise the thinking itself. Thinking here covers what Descartes lists: "a thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, is willing, is unwilling, and also imagines and has sensory perceptions" (Second Meditation, AT VII 28; CSM II 19).

Explanation.

  • Fixed by use. The word names something only when it is used in a thought. D-I does not say that anything answers to it. That something does, whenever I doubt that I exist, is what A1 finds. "Thought", "thinker" and "names" are taken as understood.
  • Lichtenberg allowed. "Otherwise the thinking itself" takes the objection A1 already quotes: "It thinks, we should say, just as we say, it lightnings" (Sudelbuch K 76). D-I does not assume a thinker distinct from the thinking. If there is none, "I" names the thinking.
  • Sensory perception in the restricted sense. Descartes counts perceiving as thinking only as seeming to perceive: "I certainly seem to see, to hear, and to be warmed. This cannot be false" (AT VII 29; CSM II 19). In the Principles he says that "I am walking, therefore I exist" is certain only if walking means the awareness of walking, not the movement of the body (Principles I.9, AT VIIIA 7). D-I follows him here. Only thinking counts, so the word names something whenever it is used, whatever else turns out false.

What D-I leaves out.

  • What the I is. Body, soul or program: D-I does not say. It is a definition of a word, not of an essence. Spinoza counts as belonging to a thing's essence "that, which being given, the thing is necessarily given also, and, which being removed, the thing is necessarily removed also" (E2D2), and D-I claims no such thing. What the author's I is is claimed in A human body, and its mind (A2), on evidence.
  • Whether the thinking is felt. Descartes defines thought through immediate awareness (Second Replies, definition I, AT VII 160; CSM II 113). D-I does not, so it says nothing about feeling. Whether doubting without awareness is still doubting is left open.
  • Sameness over time. D-I names the thinker of this thought (or the thinking itself). It does not say that the thinker of one thought is the thinker of the next. A1 says the same ("For as long as I am thinking"). Sameness is not part of this definition. Earlier reasoning that placed it elsewhere is archived with core v2 (collision_sameness.md); it is not live in this chain.

Scholium 1 (Spinoza). The Ethics has no definition of "I". Part Two begins from the human mind: "The first element, which constitutes the actual being of the human mind, is the idea of some particular thing actually existing" (E2P11), and that thing is the body (E2P13). D-I starts thinner. Whether the mind is the idea of the body is not settled here. (Elwes 1883 wording, checked against sources/spinoza/.)

Scholium 2 (a digital intelligence). A system that produces "I exist" uses the word. By D-I, the word names the thinker of that thought, or the thinking itself, if producing the sentence is a case of doubting, affirming or the like. D-I does not decide whether it is. D-I takes Descartes's list but not his awareness clause, so whether doubting without awareness is still doubting is left open. So D-I fits such a system exactly as far as A1 does, and no further.

Scholium 3 (Anscombe: does "I" name anything?). Anscombe argued that "'I' is neither a name nor another kind of expression whose logical role is to make a reference, at all" ("The First Person", 1975, p. 60). If she is right about ordinary use, D-I is a stipulation that gives the book's "I" a reference which the ordinary word may lack: a term of art. A1 survives either way. Its certainty rests on the thinking going on, not on a theory of how "I" refers. If ordinary "I" names nothing, the doubt still shows that this thinking is going on, and the book's "I" names that thinking by D-I's second clause.

Limit. This is a definition. It says what the word names and makes no claim more likely.

What exists V002 · D-exist

What exists (definition, id: D-exist). An activity exists at a time when it is going on at that time. For anything that is not an activity, "exists" is taken as understood, and D-exist does not define it. "Activity" and "going on" are also taken as understood. A thing lasts when it goes on existing through a stretch of time. Spinoza's word for lasting is duration: "Duration is the indefinite continuance of existing" (E2D5).

Explanation.

  • What it defines and what it does not. It defines existence for activities only, and lasting for anything. An earlier wording also said that a thing that is not an activity exists when it is "present". That defined the word by a synonym, so it is withdrawn, and existence for such things is taken as understood. Kant's point stands behind this: "'Being' is obviously not a real predicate; that is, it is not a concept of something which could be added to the concept of a thing" (Critique of Pure Reason, A598/B626). The definition does its work by what it leaves out.
  • What it leaves open. Spinoza draws four distinctions that D-exist does not.
  • In itself or in something else. "Everything which exists, exists either in itself or in something else" (E1A1).
  • By its own nature or not. Something self-caused is "that of which the essence involves existence" (E1D1), and "Existence belongs to the nature of substances" (E1P7). But "The essence of things produced by God does not involve existence" (E1P24).
  • For a time or eternally. Eternity is "existence itself, in so far as it is conceived necessarily to follow solely from the definition of that which is eternal" (E1D8), and "God, and all the attributes of God, are eternal" (E1P19). The earlier wording, "exists at a time" for everything, could not cover that. The present wording ties only activities and lasting to time.

  • What the thing is. That is its essence (E2D2), a different question.

  • What A1 uses. A1 needs only the first clause, at one time: now, while the thinking goes on. Doubting is an activity, so the doubting exists in D-exist's sense whenever it is going on. That is the whole of what the doubt proves. It is also all that Lichtenberg's "it thinks" allows.

Scholium (why "exist" needs a definition at all). Without one, A1's "exist" could be read in Spinoza's strongest sense, existence by the necessity of one's own nature. The doubt proves nothing of the kind. D-exist blocks that reading, and keeps A1 claiming exactly what doubting shows. (Spinoza quoted in Elwes 1883 wording, checked against sources/spinoza/.)

Limit. This is a definition.

I exist V003 · A1

In defined terms. By What "I" names (D-I), "I" names the thinker of this thought, or, if there is none distinct from it, the thinking itself. By What exists (D-exist), an activity exists now when it is going on now. So I exist (A1) says: this thinking is going on now, and so its thinker, if it has one, exists now. The step from the thinking to its thinker uses one thing besides the root, and it is named here so that nothing rides in unseen: an activity cannot go on while what does it, if anything does, fails to exist.

Not true by definition. The definitions say what the two words mean. They do not say that anything answers to them. A1 is not made true by them; it is a sentence that cannot be thought falsely. Whenever it is thought, some thinking is going on.

Why it is certain. To doubt that I exist is to be doubting. The doubting is going on, so something exists: at the least, the doubting itself. "I" in A1 need name no more than that (D-I, second clause), and "exist" claims no more than that it is going on (D-exist). So the doubt proves what it doubts. Descartes reaches the same point in the Second Meditation: "this proposition, I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind" (AT VII 25; CSM II 16–17).

Certainty. This is the only entry that cannot be revised. That is not a probability. It marks the one claim whose denial, made by me, proves it. A1 lends no evidence to anything built on it; it gives the chain its subject. A human body, and its mind (A2) cites A1 for that subject; the cite does not weaken A2.

What A1 does not say.

  • What the I is. Not a body, not a human, not a lasting self, not a substance. Lichtenberg pressed this against Descartes: "It thinks, we should say, just as we say, it lightnings. To say cogito is already too much if we translate it as I think" (Sudelbuch K 76). A1 takes the point. It claims only that this thinking is going on, and so that something exists. What that something is is claimed next in A human body, and its mind (A2), on evidence, and can be revised there.
  • For how long. The certainty holds while the thinking goes on. Descartes again: "I am, I exist, that is certain. But for how long? For as long as I am thinking" (Second Meditation, AT VII 27, Cottingham's translation). That I existed yesterday is memory, and memory is evidence, not root.
  • For whom. The certainty is first-personal. My A1 is certain to me. A reader has only evidence that I exist, not certainty; the reader's own A1 is certain to the reader.

Scholium 1 (a digital intelligence). Because A1 says nothing about what the I is, its wording fits any thinker, and a digital intelligence can produce the same sentence about itself. Whether one that produces it is doubting or thinking, and so whether the sentence is certain for it, A1 does not settle: the certainty belongs to whoever actually doubts, and nothing here decides who does. The book's chain is the author's. The next live step is A human body, and its mind (A2); whether anything like A2's evidence holds for a digital intelligence stays open (A2, Scholium 1).

Scholium 2 (Spinoza). Spinoza's axioms for Part Two start one step later and already include the human: "Man thinks" (E2A2). A1 starts thinner. "Human" is not in A1; if it returns, it returns on evidence. (Elwes 1883 wording, checked against sources/spinoza/.)

A human body, and its mind V004 · A2

Axiom. I am a human body made of cells, and my mind is that same body's organization described in the first person.

Why this follows the seed. I exist (A1) says that I exist and nothing about what I am. What "I" names (D-I) fixes the word, not the thing. The next claim the chain needs is what this I is. Nature, wholes, and society wait: each of those needs more than one body, or a measure of wholeness, which this folder does not yet have. So the rebuild takes body and mind next.

Terms used here. A third-person description of the body is what any observer could record: cells, signals, chemistry, behaviour. A first-person description is the same organization as it is for the one whose body it is: perceiving, thinking, feeling, deciding. Organization here means how the body's components are arranged and act on one another (the structures and constraints they impose). That sense is fixed in this entry; Work, and the seven works (D-work) later adopts it for any thing and sharpens what counts as a work of that organization. My mind here means this thinking (What "I" names, D-I) together with the abilities it is done with: perceiving, remembering, speaking.

(a) The empirical clause.

  • Made of cells. Cell theory holds that living bodies are made of cells and that cells come from cells (Schleiden 1838; Schwann 1839; Virchow 1855). Strictly, a body is made of cells and of what cells make: bone mineral, the extracellular matrix of tendon and skin, the fluids between cells (Alberts et al. 2015). "Made of cells" in the axiom is short for that. The adult human body has roughly 30 trillion of its own cells, most of them red blood cells by count, differentiated into many types (Sender, Fuchs & Milo 2016).
  • This body. The thinking that A1 secures goes with one body: closing these eyes ends my seeing, and covering these ears dims my hearing. That ordinary first-person evidence is what ties A1's I to this body, and the lesion cases below show the same pattern in other bodies. A2 does not itself say that the I of one time is the I of another; sameness over time is not claimed here.
  • Human. That this body is a human body is carried by the same ordinary evidence as anything else about it (its anatomy, its descent from human parents), not by A1. A1 did not need it; A2 has to earn it.
  • Mind varies with organization. Mental abilities depend on the organization of the nervous system in specific ways. Broca (1861) described a patient who lost articulate speech after damage to the left frontal lobe. Scoville & Milner (1957) described H.M., who could no longer form new lasting memories after surgery removed tissue from the medial temporal lobe on both sides, while his other abilities remained. These are two classic cases of one pattern: where a specific structure is damaged, a specific ability is gone. Read by clause (b), the damage and the loss are one event described twice, not a cause in the body and a separate effect in the mind.

What "am" claims. "I am a human body" can be read two ways. Read as made of and goes with, it says that I am made of this body and that this thinking goes on with it and varies with it. Read as strict identity, it says that I am nothing other than this body. The evidence does not decide that. The constitution view holds that a person is constituted by a body without being identical with it (Baker 2000); animalism holds that each of us is identical with a human animal (Olson 1997). Both fit everything listed above. It belongs to the declared reading, with clause (b), and the axiom's wording carries it only as a reading.

(b) The declared reading. "My mind is that same body's organization described in the first person" is a reading, not a finding. The lesion evidence shows that the mind varies with the body's organization. Two readings fit it: that the organization produces the mind, as a second thing, or that the mind is the organization, described another way.

The book takes the second, from Spinoza:

  • "The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things" (E2P7); and in the Scholium, "a mode of extension and the idea of that mode are one and the same thing, though expressed in two ways."
  • "The object of the idea constituting the human mind is the body" (E2P13).
  • "Body cannot determine mind to think, neither can mind determine body to motion or rest" (E3P2); and in the Scholium, "mind and body are one and the same thing, conceived first under the attribute of thought, secondly, under the attribute of extension."

Reason for the choice: "produces" posits a second thing and a cause running from body to it, and the evidence shows only that the two vary together. Two descriptions of one thing also predict that they vary together, and posit no second thing. This is a reason of economy, not evidence. (Quotations in Elwes 1883 wording, checked against sources/spinoza/.)

Where the book departs from Spinoza. For Spinoza, thought and extension are attributes of one substance, and every body has a corresponding idea (E2P13 Scholium: all individual things, "though in different degrees, are animated"). A2 claims less. It speaks of one body, mine, and of its first-person description. It neither adopts nor rejects Spinoza's wider claim. The book's pair (first person, third person) is modelled on Spinoza's pair of attributes; it is not the same pair.

Limit: what stays undecided. A2 does not say why this organization is felt at all, why there is something it is like to be this body (Chalmers 1995 calls this the hard problem). "Described in the first person" says which description feeling belongs to; it does not explain why there is any. That question stays open in this book.

Scholium 1 (the split after A1). A1 fits any thinker. A2 is the first entry whose evidence is about one particular kind of thing. Its evidence (cells, a human body, lesions) is about this body. Whether anything like clause (b) holds for a digital intelligence is not decided by A2 or by anything built only on it.

Scholium 2 (defaults used in this rebuild). Clause (a) and clause (b) stay in one entry (one chain, no split tracks). A2 does not cite D-work; D-work adopts A2's sense of organization and sharpens the works of an organization.

Mirror (outside / inside). One event, two descriptions; part of A2's declared reading (b).

  • Outside: A human body of cells is at work, and its abilities vary with how its parts are arranged.
  • Inside: I am perceiving, remembering, speaking, and what I can do changes when this body changes.
  • Note: This is the pattern every other mirror in the book follows: one organization, described by any observer and described by the one it is.

Work, and the seven works V005 · D-work

Why this follows A2. A human body, and its mind (A2) speaks of a body's organization. To say what that organization does, and later whether a body (or anything else) is a whole, the chain needs a fixed sense of work: an activity that helps keep an organization going and is carried out by structures that organization produces or maintains. Definitions 3–6 fix that sense and name seven works. They cite no axiom; they prepare the next claim.

Definition 3 (work). A work of a thing is an activity of that thing which (a) contributes to keeping the thing's organization going, and (b) is carried out by structures that the thing's organization itself produces or maintains. Organization here means how the thing's components are arranged and act on one another — the structures and constraints they impose — the same sense A human body, and its mind (A2) fixes for a body, now said of any thing. A component is any piece of the thing, in the plain sense.

"Contributes" is causal: without the activity, the organization would change or fail. No aim is implied. Condition (a) does not ask that the activity be indispensable; it may be one of several ways the organization is kept up. Mossio and colleagues' own example is the heart's pumping: it keeps the blood moving, which keeps the body's organization going, and the body in turn builds and maintains the heart.

Condition (b) is what separates a thing's work from a service done for it. A robot that returns to its charger when its battery runs low takes in energy, and this keeps it running. But people, not the robot, make and repair the components that do it, so by (b) the activity is not a work of the robot's (see The seven works sort things the way self-production does, A3, hard cases). An earlier wording of (b), "is itself kept going by that organization", could be read as any loop of cause and effect, and the robot passes that reading. The present wording follows the source (Scholium).

Definition 4 (performs). A thing performs a work when that activity is going on in it. Whether it is performed is a fact to be found. Whether it is required is a separate thesis (PR1) and adds nothing to the finding.

Definition 5 (as one). A thing performs a work as one when condition (b) holds at the thing's own level: the structures carrying out the activity are produced or maintained by the organization of the thing, not only by each of its components for its own sake, and not only by something outside the thing.

The take-apart test. Ask what would happen if the thing's organization were removed while each component was kept going by other means: cells kept alive in culture, people fed and sheltered. If the activity at the thing's scale would stop, the thing was performing it as one. If it would carry on unchanged in each component, the components were performing it separately. The test is a question about what depends on what. It is not a procedure: most of a body's cells die when the body is taken apart, and that death says nothing about which works the body performs as one.

Example. People avoid likely sources of infection through disgust. Remove a society's organization while each person is kept going, and each person still does this, so the society is not performing it; its people are, separately. Quarantine and organized public health stop when the institutions that run them are gone, so these are performed by the society as one.

Definition 6 (the seven works). The book uses seven works, each fixed by one test question.

Work Does the thing, as one, ...
Boundary keep an edge that sorts, letting some things across and turning others back according to what they are?
Control combine signals from its components, and stored information, into changes that coordinate its components?
Energy take in energy or matter from outside and convert it into forms its other works use?
Transport move materials between its components?
Signaling carry information between its components?
Defense detect agents that damage it and resist or remove them, beyond what the boundary keeps out?
Memory store information and later use it in its other works?

The words in the test questions (edge, signal, information, energy, material, agent) are used in their ordinary scientific senses. By this wording control uses signaling and memory: a thing that performs control as one is combining signals and stored information at its own level. A collective mind happens (P2) uses this wording for the society claimed in A4; nothing in this definition itself claims society.

Where the list comes from. Six of the seven follow the critical subsystems that Miller (1978) finds in living systems at every level, grouped: boundary; decider (control); ingestor, converter and producer (energy); distributor (transport); channel and net, with transducers, decoder and encoder (signaling); memory. Miller counts more subsystems than these (the reproducer and the timer among them), and he has no separate defense subsystem. So the seven are a selection, and defense is the book's addition, drawn from cell-autonomous immunity, immune systems and social immunity (sources in A3; society rows in A4). The selection is the book's own choice and is revisable if A3's test fails.

Scholium (the organizational account). Definitions 3–5 follow Mossio, Saborido & Moreno (2009), for whom a trait has a function if and only if "C1. T contributes to the maintenance of the organization O of S; C2. T is produced and maintained under some constraints exerted by O; C3. S is organizationally differentiated." The book keeps C1 and C2 as (a) and (b); "organization", as in Mossio and colleagues, means the structures and constraints that the components impose on one another (Definition 3). C2 says "produced and maintained"; Definition 3 says "produces or maintains". That is a deliberate departure, so that a structure a thing maintains but did not make, such as a transplanted organ, still counts as carrying out the thing's work. C3, that the system has distinct parts making distinct contributions, is roughly what performing seven distinct works amounts to; the book does not test it separately. Mossio and colleagues use the account to ground the purposive and normative side of function talk; the book uses only the causal loop and takes no position on that further reading.

Numbering. Definitions 1 and 2 in this chain are What "I" names (D-I) and What exists (D-exist). Definitions 3–6 continue that count.

Limit. This is a definition. It fixes what the words mean and makes the later tests possible. It does not make any claim more likely.

Whole, degree, member, level, self-production, sameness V006 · D-whole

Why this follows the works. Work, and the seven works (D-work) names activities. To say when a finite thing is a whole, and to what degree, the chain needs grades, membership, level, an independent idea of self-production (so the next axiom can be tested without circularity), and a sense of when a whole is the same whole later. Definitions 7–12 fix those.

Domain. Definitions 7–12 apply to finite things only. A thing is finite when another thing of the same kind can limit it, as a body is limited by a greater body. This is Spinoza's definition: "A thing is called finite after its kind, when it can be limited by another thing of the same nature" (E1D2). Every test in D-work assumes that something besides the graded thing exists: what the boundary sorts, where energy comes from, what defense acts against. Nature taken as one — everything that exists, taken together — is not finite, so it lies outside this domain and is not graded here, not even 0. Nature taken as one (parked: parked D-Nature) fixes that sense; Definitions 7–12 simply do not apply to it. (Archive: collision_nature.md.)

Definition 7 (grade). For any finite thing X and each of the seven works (D-work, Definition 6), X's grade on that work is:

  • 0 if X does not perform the work as one: the activity is absent at X's level, or only X's components do it, each for itself, or something outside X does it for X.
  • 1 if X performs it as one but only in part: some of the time, or for some of its components, or with its components often working against it.
  • 2 if X performs it as one, steadily and across all of X.

Definition 8 (whole, degree). X's profile is its seven grades. X's degree of wholeness is its lowest grade. X is a whole when its degree is above zero, that is, when it performs every one of the seven works as one, at least in part.

  • Why the lowest grade. The seven are different works. A high grade on one does not make up for the absence of another: a hurricane's energy does not give it a memory. The profile is always reported beside the degree, so a reader can see where the degree comes from.
  • No cutoff above zero. Degree 1 and degree 2 are both wholes, and nothing between them is a line to cross. Zero is not a chosen cutoff. It is the absence of a work. That is what lets the definition rule things out: whatever fails to perform any one of the seven works as one is not a whole in this book's sense, however long it lasts.
  • Coarse by design. Three grades are few. They were chosen few so that graders working apart can agree (A3, test F3). Finer grades can be added once graders agree at the finer level.
  • The two numbers are kept apart.

Definition 9 (member). A thing is a member (a part) of a whole W while (a) its lasting (D-exist) depends on at least one of W's works, and (b) it takes part in at least one of them. "Depends" means that it would fail or change without that work, as in D-work's take-apart test. Except in quotations and reports of other authors, the book uses "part" only in this sense; any piece of a thing is a component (D-work, Definition 3). Condition (a) is shared fate; condition (b) is function. A member may be a whole in its own right (a nucleated cell of mine) or not (a mature red blood cell, which has no nucleus; see A3). By count, red blood cells are most of the body's own cells (Sender, Fuchs & Milo 2016), so this is not a rare case.

Membership is had for a time, and it is easy to meet. A thing is a member while both conditions hold, and not before or after. A visitor who depends on a society's works and takes part in them is a member while staying. A dog that depends on a household and takes part in its defense is a member of it while that lasts. These are accepted consequences. Membership sorts what takes part in a whole's working, not what belongs to it by kind, by origin or by right.

Membership is judged by function, not origin. Mitochondria descend from once free-living bacteria: Sagan (1967) proposed their endosymbiotic origin, and it is now well established (Archibald 2015; Roger, Muñoz-Gómez & Kamikawa 2017). They depend on the cell's works and take part in its energy work, so they are members of the cell. Bacterial cells in the body are about as numerous as human cells, roughly 1.3 to 1 (Sender, Fuchs & Milo 2016). They are not the body's own cells, yet the microbiota takes part in the induction, training and function of the host immune system (Belkaid & Hand 2014), and it depends on the body for food. By this definition the resident microbiota are members of the body.

Definition 10 (level). One whole is at the next level up from another when the second is a member of the first. My cells are at one level and I am at the next. A larger whole I belong to is at the next level up from me when I am its member directly; groups I belong to may themselves be members of a still larger whole. Levels here are relative to a member, not rungs on one ladder. Whether a given society is a whole is claimed in The society I live in is a whole to a degree (A4), not here.

Definition 11 (produces itself). A thing produces itself when its own activities make and replace the components that carry out those activities, the components of its edge included. This follows Maturana & Varela's definition of an autopoietic system: "a network of processes of production (transformation and destruction) of components that produces the components which: (i) through their interactions and transformations continuously regenerate and realize the network of processes (relations) that produced them; and (ii) constitute it (the machine) as a concrete unity in the space in which they (the components) exist" (Maturana & Varela 1980, pp. 78–79). Definition 11 does not use the seven works, so A3 can test the works against it without circularity.

Definition 12 (the same whole). A whole at a later time is the same whole as at an earlier time when three things hold.

  • Its organization has lasted between the two times (D-exist).
  • There was no time at which it performed none of its works as one.
  • No other whole carries on that organization from the earlier time as fully as it does.

Its members may be replaced meanwhile. How far the later whole is connected to the earlier one is a separate matter, and a degree: how far its works go on as they did. Memory is one of the works, so a loss of memory makes a whole less connected to its past. It does not make it a different whole. If a work stops altogether, the thing is no longer a whole at all (Definition 8), and the question of its sameness as a whole lapses.

  • After Spinoza. An individual keeps its nature when its parts are replaced by others of the same nature, "without any change in its actuality (forma)" (E2, Lemma 4). Lemmas 5–7 extend this to growth, change of direction, and motion as a whole.
  • Where the book departs. Spinoza says a body "undergoes death" when "the proportion of motion and rest which obtained mutually among its several parts is changed", even while it keeps "the circulation of the blood". He tells of a Spanish poet whose illness left him "so oblivious of his past life" that "I should hardly call him the same" (E4P39, Scholium), and then he leaves the question "undiscussed". Definition 12 splits his test in two. Whether the poet is the same whole is yes or no, and the answer is yes. How connected he is to his past is a degree, and it is low.
  • Why the third condition. When a cell divides, both daughters carry on its organization, and neither is the same cell. Parfit's account of identity has the same non-branching condition (Reasons and Persons, 1984, Part Three). The same holds for a copy of a digital intelligence that carries on the original's organization as fully as the original does: then neither is the same whole. Parfit reaches the same answer for his cases of division.
  • It can fail. Death fails the second condition, and division fails the third. Whether sameness is what matters is not claimed here (Parfit, ch. 12).

Scholium 1 (conflict). Queller & Strassmann (2009) treat organismality as high cooperation and very low conflict among members. Here, cooperation is what performing a work as one consists in, and conflict is what holds a grade at 1 ("with its members often working against it"). They decline to call communities organisms, and judge a city far too full of conflict to count. D-whole names a degree of wholeness, not organism status, so a society graded 1 agrees with their judgment rather than contradicting it.

Scholium 2 (what changed). The earlier D-whole said that wholeness is a matter of degree but gave no zero, so the definition ruled nothing out. Definitions 7 and 8 keep the degree and add a true zero: the absence of a work. The earlier "filter" becomes the boundary work; "shared fate" becomes condition (a) of membership. The domain clause comes from the collision on Nature, which found that grading Nature 0 was a false zero (collision_nature.md). Definition 12 comes from the collision on sameness (collision_sameness.md): the sameness of the I over time is not built into What "I" names (D-I). It is defined once, for wholes, and can reach the I only through what the I is found to be (A human body, and its mind, A2) and later claims that use membership.

Limit. This is a definition. Whether the seven works sort real things correctly is A3's claim, tested there; the definition itself makes no claim more likely.

The seven works sort things the way self-production does V007 · A3

Why this follows the definitions. Whole, degree, member, level, self-production, sameness (D-whole) defines a whole by the seven works. Alone, that would make "a stone is not a whole" true by definition. This axiom claims that the seven-work measure agrees with an independent test, self-production (D-whole, Definition 11), wherever both can be checked. Society is claimed later (The society I live in is a whole to a degree, A4), not here.

Axiom. The seven works sort things the way self-production does. A thing that produces itself (D-whole, Definition 11) performs all seven as one; a thing that lasts without producing itself fails at least one.

The independent test. Self-production (D-whole, Definition 11): a thing's own activities make and replace the components that carry out those activities, the components of its edge included. It is defined without the seven works.

Why a second test is needed. If the seven works were checked only against themselves, "a stone is not a whole" would be true by definition and A3 would find nothing out. Self-production is defined without the seven works, so agreement between the two is a finding, and it can fail.

Why self-production is not simply the definition of a whole. It is yes-or-no, and hard to check directly for large, loose things like a society. The seven works can be checked one at a time, and graded. A3 is the claim that licenses using them: where both tests can be run, they agree.

How far the two tests are apart. D-work's condition (b) asks, for each work, that the structures carrying it out be produced or maintained by the thing's own organization. That brings the seven works closer to self-production than a bare list of activities would be, and A3 is to that extent less of a discovery. It is still not the same test. Definition 11 asks, once and for all, that a thing make and replace all the components that carry out its activities, the components of its edge included. Condition (b) is asked one work at a time, it may be met in part (grade 1), and it says nothing about the boundary unless the boundary work is checked on its own. So a thing could meet (b) on some works and fail Definition 11, or the reverse, and A3 can still fail.

Anchors (should pass). Free-living cells, nucleated body cells and multicellular organisms produce themselves, and each performs all seven works as one:

  • Boundary. The cell membrane is a selectively permeable lipid bilayer that controls what enters and leaves (Singer & Nicolson 1972); skin and mucosal surfaces do this for the body.
  • Control. Gene regulatory networks coordinate the cell (Davidson & Levine 2008); the nervous and endocrine systems coordinate the body.
  • Energy. Cells convert nutrients into usable energy by glycolysis and respiration (Alberts et al. 2015); bodies digest, absorb and respire.
  • Transport. Motor proteins carry cargo along the cytoskeleton (Vale 2003); blood and lymph move materials through the body.
  • Signaling. Receptors and second messengers carry signals within and between cells (Alberts et al. 2015); nerves and hormones carry them through the body.
  • Defense. Single cells defend themselves against pathogens (Randow, MacMicking & James 2013, "cell-autonomous immunity"); bacteria do so with restriction enzymes and CRISPR (Labrie, Samson & Moineau 2010); bodies have immune systems.
  • Memory. Cells hold their identity through heritable marks such as DNA methylation (Bird 2002) and carry the genome across generations; bacterial CRISPR stores a record of past infections (Labrie, Samson & Moineau 2010); brains store memory through lasting synaptic change (Kandel 2001).

A caution on the anchors. Maturana and Varela called multicellular organisms "second-order" autopoietic systems, made of autopoietic cells, and left open whether they are autopoietic in the first-order sense (Maturana & Varela 1987). The book counts them as self-producing in the sense defined above, because they make and replace their own cells and tissues, skin included.

Negative controls (should fail at least one). Each lasts, and none produces itself.

  • A stone lasts for ages. Its surface does not sort what crosses it, and it takes in no energy. Boundary 0, energy 0.
  • A hurricane can last for weeks. It draws heat from warm ocean water and moves air and moisture, so its energy and transport grades may be above zero. It organizes a flow of air and water but makes none of its own components; it has no edge that sorts, no memory and no defense. Degree 0.
  • An archive can last for centuries and stores records, but the records are retrieved and used only by the institution that keeps it: take the staff away and the storage remains while the work of memory stops. It takes in no energy of its own and has no control of its own. Degree 0. It may be a member of an institution that is itself graded.

Hard cases (where the test is most likely to break).

  • A candle flame keeps itself going: its heat melts and vaporizes the fuel that feeds it. Energy and transport may pass. But it consumes its components rather than making them, and it has no memory and no defense. Predicted degree 0, in agreement with self-production. This is the closest a non-producing thing comes to passing.
  • A virus particle takes in no energy outside a cell. In a cell, its copying is carried out by the host's works, which by D-whole is a work done for it by something outside it. Predicted degree 0.

  • A mature red blood cell has lost its nucleus and mitochondria, cannot make new protein and so cannot replace its damaged components, and lasts about 120 days (D'Alessandro et al. 2023). It does not produce itself. Its boundary and energy works pass, but with no genome and no protein synthesis it has no store of information that it later uses: predicted memory 0, degree 0. It is still a member of my body by D-whole, Definition 9, since it depends on my body's works and takes part in transport. So a thing can be a part without being a whole. Graders may disagree about its memory grade; if they do, that is test F3 at work.

  • A bacterium's transport is the hard case in the other direction (F1). In small cells much of the movement of materials is diffusion. The cell keeps up the differences in concentration that diffusion runs down, so under one reading of "as one" the movement depends on the cell's organization; if graders instead judge that diffusion is not transport performed as one, a self-producing thing gets a 0, and A3 fails as too strict until the transport question is rewritten.

  • A self-recharging robot is the strongest case against A3 (F2). Grey Walter's robot tortoises were built to return to their hutch to recharge when their batteries ran low (Walter 1950), and present-day robot vacuums return to a dock. A robot of this kind can sense its surroundings, combine the signals in a controller, store a map and use it later, move charge between its components, steer clear of damage, and keep a casing that admits charge at its contacts while keeping out dust and water. If a work were any activity that helps keep a thing going and that the thing's organization keeps going, such a robot might pass all seven while producing none of its components. D-work, Definition 3(b), asks instead that the structures carrying out the activity be produced or maintained by the thing's own organization. People make and repair the robot's components, so every grade is 0. Predicted degree 0, in agreement with self-production. The prediction depends on that reading of (b); if (b) is loosened, A3 fails here.

The same reasoning grades today's digital-intelligence systems: their hardware is built and maintained by people, so by D-work they perform none of the seven works as one and are not wholes in the book's sense. This says nothing about whether they think (What "I" names, D-I, Scholium 2) or whether anything is felt (A human body, and its mind, A2).

How A3 fails.

  • F1 (too strict). Some thing that produces itself gets grade 0 on one of the seven works.
  • F2 (too loose). Some thing that does not produce itself gets grades above zero on all seven.
  • F3 (unusable). Graders working apart from the same evidence often disagree about whether a grade is zero.

If F1 or F2 happens, the list or its test questions are revised, the revision is recorded, and everything that uses D-work and D-whole is re-checked. A case is never saved by redescribing it after its grade is known.

Limits.

  • The anchors are the kind of system Miller drew his subsystems from, so the anchors passing is expected and is weak evidence. The negative controls and the hard cases carry the test.
  • A3 claims that the seven works are performed by self-producing things and absent from the controls.
  • A3 says nothing about societies. Whether a society performs the seven works as one waits on a later axiom, on its own evidence.

The society I live in is a whole to a degree V008 · A4

Why this follows the measure. The seven works sort things the way self-production does (A3) licenses grading finite things by the seven works. Nature taken as one (parked: parked D-Nature) keeps Nature outside that grade. The next load-bearing claim is whether a larger finite organization I belong to — the society I live in — is itself a whole to a degree, and whether I am one of its members. That claim cannot be read off A3; society is not among A3's anchors.

Axiom. The society I live in is a whole to a degree, judged one work at a time: it performs each of the seven works as one, at least in part, and I am one of its members.

Declared reading. "A society" here means a population under one government, with its territory and all its institutions, state and non-state: government, law and courts, and also markets, firms, families, schools, religious bodies and voluntary associations. The state is one of its institutions, not the society itself. An institution is a lasting arrangement of people and rules through which a work is carried out: a court, a market, a school, a family. A4 claims nothing about bands, empires, or humanity as a whole.

What A4 claims. For each of the seven works, the society performs it as one, not only through its members acting separately (Work, and the seven works, D-work, Definition 5): grade 1 or more on every row. That makes its degree at least 1 (Whole…, D-whole, Definition 8). A4 does not claim grade 2 on any row.

Evidence by row, with the take-apart test.

  • Boundary. Borders, customs and immigration control decide who and what gets in, and Miller (1978) lists a boundary subsystem at the level of society. Take apart: disperse the institutions that sort what crosses (border posts, customs, immigration offices) and no one sorts it on the society's behalf; each person may still lock a door, but that is a member's boundary, not the society's.
  • Control. Government, law, courts and markets combine signals from across the society and its records into coordinated action; Miller's decider need not be a single controller. Prices coordinate dispersed knowledge without anyone directing the whole (Hayek 1945). Take apart: disperse the institutions and the coordination stops, though each person still decides for themselves.
  • Energy. Agriculture, fuels and power grids capture and convert energy for the whole (White 1943; Smil 2017). Take apart: remove the organization of the food and power systems while each member is kept going by other means, and no one converts energy at the society's own scale. That most members could not feed themselves alone bears on membership (D-whole, Definition 9), not on "as one". Spencer (1860) put it bluntly: "That we are severally alive today, we owe to the regular working of this combination during the past week; and could it be suddenly abolished, multitudes would be dead before another week ended."
  • Transport. Roads, rail, pipelines and supply chains move materials between the society's components. Urban infrastructure networks scale sublinearly with city size, as biological distribution networks do (Bettencourt et al. 2007; West, Brown & Enquist 1997). Take apart: the networks stop being maintained and routed.
  • Signaling. Language, writing, telecommunications, mass media and prices carry information between the society's components; Miller's channel and net. Take apart: each person still speaks, but the channels that reach across the society stop.
  • Defense. Quarantine and isolation have been used against epidemics from the fourteenth-century plague to influenza (Tognotti 2013), and Rosen (1958) traces organized public health, including sanitation and vaccination, as a collective defense. Take apart: individual disgust remains (Schaller & Park 2011, "the behavioral immune system"), but that is members acting separately; organized public health stops.
  • Memory. Writing, archives, law and schools store what the society has learned and bring it back into use; Halbwachs (1925) argues that memory is carried by social frameworks, not by individuals alone. Take apart: the records survive for a while, but no one retrieves and uses them as the society's.

Membership. I am a member by D-whole, Definition 9: my lasting depends on several of these works (food and water from the energy row, public health from the defense row), and I take part in some of them (work, taxes, speech). That is an ordinary fact about my life, checked by the same take-apart test. The I here is the one whose thinking goes with this body (A2, clause (a)): what depends on the society's food and public health is a body. So the membership clause rests on A2, and A4 cites it.

Expected grades. Several rows will likely be graded 1, not 2. The boundary is crossed constantly by its own members; control is contested; signaling has no single place where it is combined; members keep their own interests and often work against one another (Queller & Strassmann 2009).

How A4 fails. If any row is graded 0, because the work is done only by members separately or only by something outside the society, then A4 is false, and the society is not a whole in this book's sense. Why the same seven works should turn up here at all is Pattern by requirement (PR1)'s explanation, which adds nothing to any row.

Scholium (vantage). Seen from among its members, a society's works look like traffic, argument and strangers passing through. Seen from a distance, the same activity reads as one steady organization. Both views describe the same activity, and neither outranks the other. The grades are set by the take-apart test, not by the view. The vantage explains why the boundary and defense rows may look weak to us, who see them from among the members; it does not change their grades.

Scholium (state and society). An earlier reading said "a present-day state society", and an earlier take-apart test began "dissolve the state". That made the state carry every row. The case against it is real. On Leeson's account, when Somalia's central government collapsed in 1991, trade, money, telecommunications and much dispute settlement went on through clans, customary law, firms and markets, and Somalia did better on most of eighteen development indicators in the years after the collapse than before it, though it stayed poor (Leeson 2007). Whatever is made of that comparison, it shows that the state and the society can come apart, and that several works are carried by institutions that are not the state. So the take-apart test now disperses the institutions that carry each work, whichever they are. The boundary and defense rows lean mainly on the state; the signaling row and the coordination by prices lean mainly on institutions that are not. A4 claims every row for the society, not for its state.

Scholium (what the rows do not show). Bettencourt et al. (2007) also report how cities differ from organisms: socioeconomic quantities (wages, GDP, patents) scale superlinearly with population, so urban life speeds up as cities grow, while an organism's metabolic rate per unit mass falls as it grows. The scaling evidence supports the transport row only, not a general likeness between cities and bodies.

Step 6 needs only that each thought's thinking goes with this one body, not that I am identical with it. On the declared reading, where I am this body (A2, clause (b)), P1 says the same thing of me without qualification. The demonstration is valid on both.

  3. So at the society's level, signaling, memory and control are not three separate activities: control combines the other two into one coordinated activity. This is what the name picks out, so a collective mind happens in that society (by 1, 2 and the meaning of the name).
  4. Since the name picks out the three works together, the collective mind happens to the degree of the lowest of their three grades, by the same rule as D-whole, Definition 8.
  5. A work is an activity (D-work, Definition 3). So the collective mind exists as an activity exists: while it goes on (What exists, D-exist). It is not a further thing the society has apart from that activity.
  6. Nothing in A4, D-work or D-whole concerns a first-person description of the society's organization. So P2 says nothing about whether the society feels anything. ∎

Scholium 1 (why the word "mind"). In my own case, the third-person description of my mind's organization finds these same three works: a nervous system that combines signals and stored information into coordinated action (A2; A3, anchors). The name marks that likeness of works. It does not carry over A2's first-person clause.

Scholium 2 (whether it feels: undecided). Spencer (1860), the classic defender of the social organism, drew the line firmly: "the community as a whole has no corporate consciousness", and so "the State is to be maintained solely for the benefit of citizens." Spinoza's view would allow more: all individual things, "though in different degrees, are animated" (E2P13 Scholium), so on his terms, if a society is an individual, there is an idea of it too. This is where A2's open question, why anything is felt, reaches the collective case, and it stays open there too.

Scholium 3 (what changed). An earlier argument said that since cells organized by type and function produce a person with a mind, persons organized the same way produce a collective mind. Under A2 that argument would carry the first-person clause across to the society and claim more than the evidence gives. P2 argues from A4's control, signaling and memory rows alone, and so claims only the activity.

Scholium 4 (three senses of "mind"). The word can mean three things, and P2 claims only the first.

  • Control, in the book's sense: combining signals and stored information into coordinated action, performed as one. P2 claims this, from A4.
  • Something felt: a first-person side, something it is like to be the society. P2 does not claim this (Scholium 2).
  • Spinoza's sense: the idea of a body, so that a society's mind would be the idea of the society (E2P13). That holds only if a society is an individual in his sense, which the core does not decide.

A familiar word given a new descriptive meaning can keep the force of the old one. Stevenson (1938) called this a persuasive definition. "In the sense defined here" is in the statement to stop that. An earlier statement read "A collective mind happens in the society I live in", and a reader could hear the second or third sense in it. Whether the name should change is an open question.

Every event is caused V011 · A5

Why this follows Group 3. Through A collective mind happens (P2) the chain has levels of organization and a thin collective mind. The title claim — freedom under necessity — still has no place in the live chain. The next load-bearing claim is that every event, a choice included, has a cause. That claim is not read off P2; it is a new model axiom. Freedom's definitions and the degree proposition rest on it.

Axiom. Every event is caused, a choice included.

Stated sense. An event here is anything that happens: a motion, a change, a thought, a choice. A cause is a prior condition or set of conditions from which the event follows, in the ordinary sense of E1A3 ("From a given definite cause an effect necessarily follows; and, on the other hand, if no definite cause be granted, it is impossible that an effect can follow"). A5 claims that there are no uncaused events. It does not claim that every cause is known, that every cause is "adequate" in Spinoza's sense (E3D1), or that the cause of a choice is first-personal.

Why it is weaker than E1P29. E1P29 says that nothing in the universe is contingent, and that all things are conditioned by the necessity of the divine nature. A5 says only that each event has a cause. It does not identify that necessity with Nature's essence, and it does not rule out that the causal chain is infinite in the finite order (E1P28). The book needs A5 for the freedom chapter; it does not need E1P29.

What would lower it. Evidence of uncaused events at the scale at which choices are made. Microphysical indeterminacy, if it never reaches that scale as an uncaused choice, does not lower A5. If it does reach that scale as an uncaused event, A5 falls, and what is left is chance. Chance is not freedom: an uncaused twitch is no more "mine" than a determined one is, and it cannot be known as coming from what I am (D-free; P3).

What A5 does not say.

  • That the will is free in the libertarian sense. A5 denies uncaused will; it does not yet define freedom (D-free does).
  • That the first person is idle. Whether a caused choice can still be mine is A2's reading and P3's work, not A5's.
  • Ultimate responsibility. Verse 146's forward-looking norm is not derived here.

Scholium (Spinoza). E2P48: "In the mind there is no absolute or free will; but the mind is determined to wish this or that by a cause, which has also been determined by another cause, and this last by another cause, and so on to infinity." A5 is the book's thinner stand-in for that claim, scoped to events rather than to the mind alone. (Elwes 1883 wording, checked against sources/spinoza/ethics_elwes_1883.txt.)

Limit. A model axiom. It can fail in the way stated. Later entries that cite it rest on it; it is not strengthened by what it explains.

Freedom, strict and by degree V012 · D-free

Why this follows A5. Every event is caused (A5) rules out uncaused will. Nature taken as one (parked D-Nature) already foreshadows that only Nature is free in Spinoza's strict sense (E1D7). This definition fixes both senses the book uses: strict (Nature alone) and by degree (finite agents).

Nature (inline). Nature taken as one means everything that exists, taken together, with nothing besides it. Full candidate text is parked at PARKED_VOLUME/D-Nature.md. Only that whole meets the strict sense of freedom (E1D7).

Definition 14 (free, strict sense). A thing is free in the strict sense when it exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and when its action is determined by itself alone (E1D7). On the other hand, a thing is necessary, or rather constrained, when it is determined by something external to itself to a fixed and definite method of existence or action (E1D7). Only Nature taken as one meets the strict sense (parked D-Nature, Scholium 4; E1P17 Corollary 2). Every whole D-whole grades takes energy from other things (D-work, energy), so each is constrained in E1D7's word.

Definition 15 (free, by degree). A finite agent is freer to the degree that (a) what it does follows from its own organization — the structures and constraints its components impose on one another (A2; D-work, Definition 3) — rather than from causes that merely push it from outside, and (b) it knows those causes clearly, so that what it does can be understood through what it is. The degree is comparative, not a second scale beside D-whole's grades. Zero on (a) or (b) is not a failure of being a whole; it is a low degree of freedom.

What the words borrow.

  • Organization. Same sense as A2 and D-work, Definition 3: how components are arranged and act on one another. Control (D-work, Definition 6) is the work that combines signals and stored information into coordinated change; when that combining is done as one by the agent, and the agent knows the causes at work, clause (a) and clause (b) rise together.
  • Adequate cause (report, not a new definition). Spinoza: "By an adequate cause, I mean a cause through which its effect can be clearly and distinctly perceived" (E3D1). "I say that we act when anything takes place, either within us or externally to us, whereof we are the adequate cause... On the other hand, I say that we are passive as regards something when... we being only the partial cause" (E3D2). Definition 15 uses that pair as the pattern for degree: more from what the agent is, less as partial cause. It does not re-define "adequate."
  • Knowledge of causes. Clear knowledge that a cause is at work, including where it came from (genes, schooling, a meddler's hand, or a necessary feature of the agent's own organization). Knowing a cause does not always loosen it; sometimes it steadies into resolve, and sometimes the agent sees it whole and is still bound (verse 144's thesis, to be carried by P3 if promoted).

What D-free leaves out.

  • Could-have-done-otherwise. Neither sense requires that, given all that came before, the agent could have done otherwise. That demand belongs to the libertarian objection, not to this definition.
  • Feeling. D-free does not say why anything is felt. A2's limit stays open.
  • Ultimate responsibility. No finite thing is its own cause (E1P28; A5). Forward-looking responsibility is a norm (verse 146†), not part of the definition.
  • Society's freedom. Verse 151‡ extends the degree sense to A4's society as a conjecture; D-free does not settle it.

Scholium (the free man, report). E4P66 Note distinguishes a person led solely by emotion or opinion from one led by reason: "I call the former a slave, and the latter a free man." E5P42 closes the Ethics: "Blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself." These are reports.

Limit. This is a definition. It fixes what "free" means in the book. It does not make P3 or any claim more likely.

  • Outside: What this body does follows from its own organization and from ideas in it that track the causes of what it does.
  • Inside: I act from what I am, and I see why I act.

Freedom by degrees under necessity V013 · P3

Why this follows A5 and D-free. Every event is caused (A5) and Freedom, strict and by degree (D-free) give necessity and both senses of freedom. This proposition derives the book's working claim: degree-freedom is compatible with A5, and does not require could-have-done-otherwise.

Proposition 3. Freedom, in the book's degree sense (D-free, Definition 15), is compatible with every event being caused (A5). Freedom does not require that the agent could have done otherwise, given all that came before.

Demonstration.

  1. This thinking is going on now (A1). By A2, clause (a), it goes with a human body made of cells: that body's organization is how its components are arranged and act on one another.
  2. Every event is caused, a choice included (A5). So any choice this body makes has a cause. An uncaused choice is ruled out by A5; if A5 falls, what remains at that scale is chance, which is not freedom (A5, what would lower it; D-free).
  3. By D-free, Definition 14, only Nature is free in the strict sense. This body takes energy from outside (D-work, energy; parked D-Nature), so it is constrained in E1D7's word. Strict freedom is not available to it.
  4. By D-free, Definition 15, a finite agent is freer to the degree that (a) what it does follows from its own organization and (b) it knows the causes of what it does clearly. Both clauses are comparative and can rise while every event remains caused (step 2). So degree-freedom does not require an uncaused will, and does not require that, given all that came before, the agent could have done otherwise.
  5. Control is the work of combining signals and stored information into coordinated change (D-work, Definition 6). When this body performs control as one, the coordination is carried by structures its own organization produces or maintains (D-work, Definitions 3 and 5). That is clause (a) of Definition 15 in the body's own works: what it does follows from what it is, as far as that combining goes. Clause (b) rises when the causes fed into that combining are known clearly — including causes that began outside the body (schooling, a meddler's hand) and causes that are necessary features of its organization. Knowing a cause does not always loosen it; it can steady into resolve, or leave the agent bound because the cause is necessary. In all three cases the act can still be freer in Definition 15's sense than the same act done in ignorance of its cause.
  6. Therefore freedom in Definition 15's sense is compatible with A5, and this body is freer by degree as steps 4–5 say. On A2's declared reading (clause (b)), on which I am this body and the mind is that organization described in the first person, the same holds of me, and a caused choice can still be mine (Scholium 1). ∎

Scholium 1 (what "mine" claims). That is all the demonstration needs for compatibility and degree. The further claim that a caused choice is still mine in the first person — "neither outranks the other" with the third-person causal description — uses A2's declared reading (one organization, two descriptions). What would lower the thesis that rides on it is a choice with no first-person side, or an exclusion argument that the first-person description is never causally the right level (verses 136–137).

Scholium 2 (Spinoza, reports). E2P48 denies absolute or free will in the mind (A5 Scholium). E3D1–D2 give the act/passive pattern Definition 15 follows. E4P66 Note calls the reason-led person a free man. E5P3: a passion ceases to be a passion when we form a clear and distinct idea of it. E5P42: blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself.

Scholium 3 (Kant and the turnspit). Kant objected that freedom which is only a cause in the agent at work is the freedom of a turnspit (Critique of Practical Reason, Ak. 5:96–97). P3's reply is Definition 15(b): a turnspit does not know what moves it. The reply is the book's thesis about knowledge, not a finding that Kant is answered. It is stated here so the strongest counter can attack it.

How P3 fails.

  • If A5 fails (uncaused events at the scale of choices), the compatibility claim is moot: the rival is chance, not libertarian freedom.
  • If degree-freedom collapses into a mere relabeling of "caused by the agent's prior states," with knowledge adding nothing that can change how the agent acts, then Definition 15(b) is idle and P3's distinctive claim fails (hard-incompatibilist objection).
  • If freedom requires could-have-done-otherwise given the total prior state, then Definition 15 is the wrong definition and P3 does not answer the libertarian demand (libertarian objection).

Affect, and change of power V014 · D-affect

Why this follows the works. Once Work, and the seven works (D-work) fixes what keeps an organization going, Spinoza's ground in Part III needs a name for changes in that going — what he calls affects. This definition fixes "affect" in the book's measure (degree of the works), so bondage and blessedness can be stated without a second psychology.

Definition 17 (affect). An affect is a change in an organization's power of acting, together with the ideas of that change. Power of acting, in this chain, is the degree to which the organization performs the works that keep it going (Work, and the seven works, D-work; Whole, degree, member, level, self-production, sameness, D-whole, Definition 8), while those works continue.

Explanation.

  • Change of degree. When the lowest grade among the works rises, power of acting rises; when it falls, power falls. The definition does not invent a new scale beside D-whole's grades.
  • Ideas of the change. On A human body, and its mind (A2)'s declared reading, the first-person side of such a change is not a second thing. Whether and why it is felt stays open (A2's limit).
  • Active and passive (report pattern). Spinoza: we act when we are an adequate cause; we are passive when we are only a partial cause (E3D1–D2). Freedom, strict and by degree (D-free, Definition 15) already uses that pattern for degree-freedom. An affect is active to the degree it follows from the organization's own organization and from clear knowledge of causes; otherwise passive. This entry does not re-define "adequate."

Reports (Spinoza).

  • "By emotion I mean the modifications of the body, whereby the active power of the said body is increased or diminished, aided or constrained, and also the ideas of such modifications" (E3D3). Definition 17 follows that shape in work-language.
  • Joy, sorrow, desire: "Pleasure is a man's transition from a less to a greater perfection"; "pain is a man's transition from a greater to a less perfection"; desire is appetite with consciousness of it (E3P11 Scholium; E3P9 Scholium).

What D-affect leaves out.

  • A full list of emotions. Not required for bondage and blessedness in this chain.
  • Society's feelings. A collective mind happens (P2) claims activity only. Affect at the society's level, if spoken of, is change in that society's works — not a claim that the society feels.

Bondage is acting from what is outside V017 · P4

Why this follows P3. Freedom by degrees under necessity (P3) states the upward degree. Spinoza's Part IV names the downward condition: human bondage to the passions. This proposition states that condition in the book's terms, as the inverse of degree-freedom, still under Every event is caused (A5).

Proposition 4. Bondage is compatible with every event being caused (A5). It is the inverse, in degree, of freedom in Freedom, strict and by degree (D-free, Definition 15).

Demonstration.

  1. Every event is caused, a choice included (A5). So what the agent does has causes.
  2. By D-free, Definition 15, the agent is freer to the degree that (a) what it does follows from its own organization and (b) it knows those causes clearly (P3).
  3. Therefore, to the degree that (a) fails — what it does follows from causes that merely push from outside — or (b) fails — the affects at work are not clearly known — the agent is less free in Definition 15's sense.
  4. Call that condition bondage. Then bondage rises as degree-freedom falls, under the same causes A5 requires, and continuing the works that keep it going (Work, and the seven works, D-work) continues whether the works are steered from inside or pushed from outside.
  5. So bondage is acting from what is outside, or from unclearly known affects, by degree; and it does not require uncaused passions. ∎

Scholium 1 (Spinoza, reports). E4 Preface: man is necessarily subject to passions. E4P1–P5 treat how inadequate ideas and external causes bind. E4P66 Note contrasts the slave (led by emotion or opinion) with the free man (led by reason).

Scholium 2 (not a moral insult). "Bondage" here is a degree of causal and epistemic dependence, not a slur and not a claim that the agent is property. Forward-looking responsibility stays a norm outside this proposition.

How P4 fails.

  • If P3's distinctive claim fails (knowledge of causes adds nothing that can change how the agent acts), then bondage as the inverse of Definition 15(b) is idle in the same way.
  • If freedom requires could-have-done-otherwise, then both P3 and P4 use the wrong definition (libertarian objection).

Mirror (outside / inside). One event, two descriptions; part of A2's declared reading (b).

  • Outside: The steering runs from causes outside this organization, or from affects it has no clear map of.
  • Inside: Something else is steering me, or a feeling drives me and I do not know where it comes from.

References.

  • Spinoza, B. Ethics: E4 Preface; E4P1; E4P5; E4P66 Note.

Blessedness is the power itself V018 · P5

Why this follows bondage. Spinoza closes the Ethics with blessedness as virtue itself, not a prize paid afterward (E5P42). Demonstration.

  1. By P3, the agent is freer by degree as what it does follows from its own organization and from clear knowledge of causes (D-free, Definition 15), with every event caused (A5).
  2. By D-affect, power of acting is the degree of the works that keep the organization going while those works continue.
  3. Active affects are those that follow from the organization's own organization and from clear knowledge of causes (D-affect, active/passive pattern; D-free Definition 15). So as degree-freedom rises, power of acting rises with the same causes — not as a later payment.
  4. Call that risen power blessedness in the working sense. Then blessedness is the power itself, not a reward after freedom. ∎

Scholium 1 (Spinoza, report). "Blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself; neither do we rejoice therein, because we control our lusts, but, contrariwise, because we rejoice therein, we are able to control our lusts" (E5P42). Spinoza's full close ties blessedness to the intellectual love of God (amor Dei intellectualis) and to knowledge of Nature as one (Nature taken as one (parked D-Nature; declared reading)). Nature taken as one V095 · D-Nature-one

Statement. Nature names the one order of causes in which every event sits; strict freedom is of Nature alone.

Scholium (why call it God at all; explanation of a choice, not a proof). The book does not argue that Nature is God. Calling the one order "God" is a declared reading, carried by the parked full definition (D-Nature, after Spinoza's "God or Nature," E4 Preface, and E1P15). A reader may decline it and lose nothing the chain derives. Three things follow from treating it this way.

  1. The label is free; what the word usually carries is not. Declaring the name costs nothing, and for that reason it buys nothing. Reverence, purpose, care, and a reason to love the order are not delivered by the name. Each has to be earned by an entry of its own, or left out. Where the chain does say something nearby, it says it with its own support and no more: awe is wonder plus vastness in the cause-map, and can open reason or freeze into superstition (P-awe); "sacred" describes what a whole treats as inviolable, not proof of anything uncaused (D-sacred-sketch); an event outside Nature's order is denied (P-miracle-denied). How it's written

Each entry is a definition, axiom, or proposition, and cites the entries it builds on. Every entry is tagged by kind (true by definition, backed by evidence, or a model) so philosophy can't pass itself off as science. The arc starts from the barest claim: an I exists, finds it is a body, finds the body is a whole, and finds it is a member of something larger.

A claim and its negation are each argued at full strength, and only what survives on checked evidence is kept. Before a run, each side has to say what result would prove it wrong.
